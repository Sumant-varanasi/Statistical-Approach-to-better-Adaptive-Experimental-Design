SLIDE 3 — Motivation and problem statement (65 s, Sumant)
Reactor loop (four boxes with arrows): Feed (A, B and inert gas through mass-flow controllers) → Fixed bed (catalyst, loaded once, at a set temperature and pressure) → Gas chromatograph (GC): the instrument that prints what came out → Operator decides tomorrow's set points. Loop line: repeats once a day; a run is ~24 h on stream, so a campaign affords 10–25 runs.
Card 1 Five knobs, one day per run: pressure, the flows of A, B and the inert, temperature · the catalyst is loaded once and never changed mid-campaign · each run costs a day, feed gas and operator time.
Card 2 Today: rules of thumb: too much waste product (poor selectivity) → go cooler; too little feed reacting (poor conversion) → go hotter · it works, but slowly, and it needs an experienced chemist on call · cost of today's way: a 25-run campaign is five weeks of reactor time and feed gas, and most early runs lose money.
Card 3 The real constraints: no knob may jump more than one step between runs (10 bar, 10 mL/min, 30 K) · the product must meet a spec; suggestions must stay inside safety ranges · runs cost money, so the campaign should earn while it learns.
Problem statement (three lines): Given: the run log the operator already keeps (set points and the GC printout of what came out). Objective: maximise profit per hour (product value − feed − compression − by-products, worked out from the GC reading), subject to a product spec, safety ranges and one allowed step per run. Deliver: the next run, the reason, the expected profit and whether another run is worth its cost — reaching the best operating point in fewer runs than the usual rules of thumb, for someone who knows neither kinetics nor machine learning.

SCRIPT: "Here is the system: a gas-phase fixed-bed reactor. Reactants A and B and an inert gas are fed through mass-flow controllers over a catalyst bed at a set temperature and pressure, and a gas chromatograph prints what came out. Five knobs can change between runs; the catalyst is loaded once. Each run takes about a day, so a campaign is ten to twenty-five runs. Today the operator tunes this by rules of thumb: too much waste product, go cooler; too little feed reacting, go hotter. It works, but slowly, and the early runs lose money. And there are real constraints: no big jumps between runs, a product spec to meet, and every run costs money. So our problem: take the run log the operator already keeps, and tell them what to run next, why, what profit to expect and whether another run is worth it — in fewer runs than the rules of thumb. Over to Parva for the literature and the method."

SLIDE 4 — Literature review (60 s, Parva)
Column A — Optimising expensive experiments. Plain subtitle: a statistical model guesses where the best experiment is; run it; update; repeat (Bayesian optimisation).
 - Box & Wilson 1951 — response-surface methodology: climb step by step along the steepest ascent; what a chemist's tuning still is.
 - Jones, Schonlau & Welch 1998 — Efficient Global Optimization: a Gaussian process (a model that predicts a value and how unsure it is) plus Expected Improvement choose the next experiment.
 - Shields et al., Nature 2021 — Bayesian optimisation beat expert chemists at reaction optimisation.
 - Taylor et al., Chem. Rev. 2023 — review: algorithms versus intuition.
Column B — Specs, step costs and the measured composition. Plain subtitle: respect product specs, keep the search near what you know, use what the instrument measures.
 - Gelbart, Snoek & Adams 2014 — unknown constraints: a product spec enters as a probability of feasibility.
 - Eriksson et al. 2019 (TuRBO) — trust regions keep the search local.
 - Folch et al. 2022 (SnAKe) — when changing the inputs costs, plan paths, not jumps.
 - Astudillo & Frazier 2019; Paulson & Lu 2022 — grey-box BO: the objective is a known function of a measured vector, learned from scratch with a Gaussian process.
Column C — Physics + data. Plain subtitle: mix textbook physics with learned corrections; choose runs that sharpen the model.
 - Psichogios & Ungar 1992; von Stosch et al. 2014 — hybrid models: mechanistic balances with learned parts.
 - Franceschini & Macchietto 2008; Waldron et al. 2019 — model-based design of experiments: runs chosen to sharpen or discriminate kinetic models, in closed loop on a heterogeneous catalyst.
Gap box: "Black-box BO sees one profit number per run. Grey-box BO sees the composition but learns it from scratch. Model-based DoE uses the kinetic model and the full GC reading, but designs runs for parameter precision, not profit, and may run any point. None combine kinetics learned from every GC reading, step-limited planning toward profit under a product spec, a catalyst that cannot change and a plain-language operator interface — benchmarked against the rules of thumb and black-box BO under the same limits. That is this project."

SLIDE 5 — Methodology I: the digital twin and the protocol (65 s, Parva)
Card "The digital twin":
 - A textbook model of the reactor: how much of each substance is left after the gas passes over the catalyst, written per square metre of catalyst (S = a_BET · W_cat), so what we learn belongs to the catalyst material, not to one loading.
 - Reaction speeds rise with temperature in the standard way (Arrhenius); more inert gas dilutes the feed; the reversible reaction's ceiling comes from thermodynamics. Profit = product sold − feed − compression − by-products, all from the GC reading.
 - Assumptions: isothermal plug flow, no pressure drop, no transport limitations, ideal gas, first-order rates (energy balance and pressure drop are on the next-steps list).
 - Two chemistries: simple (A + B → C, A → D) and complex (A + B ⇌ C exothermic, C + B → E over-reaction, A → D cracking): three reactions competing; only ~2 % of the operating box makes any profit.
 - The virtual reactor we test on is deliberately imperfect: the product slows the reactions (Langmuir–Hinshelwood inhibition, with a reactant term the advisor never has) and the GC has 1 % error; the new ageing scenario adds a catalyst that loses activity.
Card "The protocol every method follows": 1 load the catalyst once (mass, BET area) — never a suggestion · 2 five screening runs an operator would do anyway (temperature ladder, pressure up, more B): identical for every method · 3 from run 6 on, the method proposes the next run: within one step of the last, inside the safety ranges, GC flow minimum respected · 4 25 runs per campaign (five weeks at one run a day); 10 campaigns per method from different starting points · 5 score: the true profit of the settings each method would recommend, as % of the best possible — known only because the reactor is simulated.
Graphic: temperature window that keeps ≥ 90 % of the best profit — simple network 72 K (592–663 K), complex 32 K (513–545 K). Caption: "On the complex chemistry the profitable temperature band is about 30 degrees wide: miss it by 20 degrees and most of the profit is gone. Hotter is faster but the equilibrium and the cracking take product away; more B converts more A but the over-reaction eats the product; pressure helps the equilibrium but costs compression; the inert dilutes everything."

SCRIPT: "We built a digital twin of the reactor: a textbook model of how much of each substance is left after the gas passes over the catalyst, per square metre of catalyst, with reaction speeds rising with temperature in the standard way, and an economic layer that turns a GC reading into profit per hour. We test on two chemistries: a simple one, and a complex one where three reactions compete — hotter is faster but the equilibrium and the cracking take product away; more B converts more A but the over-reaction eats the product. The profitable temperature band is about thirty degrees wide. The virtual reactor we test against is deliberately imperfect: the product slows the reactions and the instrument has one per cent error, things the advisor's model does not know. Every method follows the same protocol: catalyst loaded once, the same five screening runs, then one allowed step per run, twenty-five runs, ten campaigns from different starting points. Because the reactor is simulated we know the true best, so every method is scored against it."

SLIDE 6 — Methodology II: how the advisor learns and decides (75 s, Parva)
Five cards (plain words; technical term once in brackets; steps 1 and 5 tagged "new this month, v5"):
 1 Fit the chemistry — find the reaction speeds that explain every GC number. Start simple; add "the product slows the reaction" or "the catalyst is ageing" only when the data clearly justify it (model ladder chosen by BIC).
 2 Keep alternatives — several chemistries that fit about equally well, not one lucky fit (posterior draws).
 3 Plan — predict profit everywhere in the operating box; find the best spot and how many allowed steps away it is.
 4 Correct — a flexible statistical model (Gaussian process) learns whatever the textbook still gets wrong, and is told how noisy the GC is, so it never chases noise.
 5 Decide — look up to four runs ahead; score each allowed path by the profit earned on the way plus what the best point found is worth for the runs left; respect the product spec (receding-horizon planner).
Table "What we tell it vs what it works out for itself": tell it = which reactions happen and their stoichiometry; the reactor's mole balances; the Arrhenius form, ideal gas, inert dilution; equilibrium from thermodynamics; prices. Works out = every reaction speed and its temperature sensitivity (activation energy); product inhibition, when the data show it; catalyst ageing, from repeated check runs; a statistical correction for whatever is still missing.
Compared against, under the same step limits: rules of thumb (our scripted encoding of the standard heuristics: poor selectivity → cooler, more pressure; poor conversion → hotter …; repeat what worked) · plain Bayesian optimisation (our own v2 package: fitted GP + Expected Improvement on the profit number only) · random walk.

SCRIPT: "The advisor's loop has five steps. One: fit the chemistry — find the reaction speeds that explain every GC number, starting simple and adding product inhibition or catalyst ageing only when the data justify it. Two: keep several chemistries that fit about equally well, not one lucky fit. Three: plan — predict profit everywhere and find the best spot and how many allowed steps away it is. Four: a flexible statistical model learns whatever the textbook still gets wrong, and it is told how noisy the instrument is, so it never chases noise. Five: decide — look a few runs ahead and score each allowed path by the profit earned on the way plus what the best point found is worth for the runs left. The split is the key idea: physics we know is typed in, what we do not know is learned from your runs. Steps one and five are this month's upgrade. Sumant will show how it performed."

LIKELY QUESTIONS (prepared answers)
- "You wrote the chemist and you wrote the reactor — isn't the advantage built in?" The rules are the textbook heuristics and they are not weak: they reach 96 % by run 25 on the simple network. The virtual reactor deliberately differs from the advisor's model (product inhibition with a reactant term the advisor never has, 1 % GC noise, and in the new scenario catalyst ageing), and the stress test removes a whole reaction. Still, it is a simulation study: the first real GC log is the test that matters, and a blind test against a chemist at the bench is next step 3.
- "Why not design of experiments?" Classical DoE / response-surface methodology (Box & Wilson 1951) fits a local polynomial and climbs along steepest ascent in steps; our rules-of-thumb baseline is essentially that done by hand, and it needs about 21 runs because it uses neither the composition nor the kinetics. Model-based DoE uses the kinetic model but designs runs to sharpen parameters or discriminate models, not to earn profit; we optimise profit while learning, and the statistical correction covers the case where the model is wrong. Model discrimination is exactly next step 2.
- "Is the planner new?" Finite-budget, non-myopic BO exists (Lam, Willcox & Wolpert, NeurIPS 2016, rollout). Ours is new in scoring trajectories of allowed steps under sampled chemistries rather than GP posteriors, and in valuing profit earned on the way.
- "Isothermal plug flow, no pressure drop, an exothermic reaction at 50 bar — what is the adiabatic temperature rise, and what about transport limitations?" Hundreds of kelvin at any useful conversion, which is why v5 reports it as a heat-load flag, not a limit. At lab scale (≤ 150 mL/min, ~1 g catalyst) the furnace holds the wall temperature and isothermal is the standard micro-reactor assumption; hot spots need an energy balance, which we plan to add after these steps. Weisz–Prater / Mears checks need the real catalyst's particle size and diffusivity — part of step 1 with Prof. Khare.
- "From a 50 K screening ladder you fit six Arrhenius parameters plus inhibition — are they identifiable?" Not all: ln k_ref and E_a are correlated over a narrow temperature range, so absolute E_a carry wide bands and we say so. The uncertainty is inflated when the physics misfits, the correction has a noise floor from the GC error, and the planner hedges over several plausible chemistries, so a wrong absolute E_a does not choose the wrong step. What is identifiable — the ordering of the activation energies and the equilibrium limit — is what sets the best temperature. Adding runs designed for parameter precision is a natural extension.
- "Why does the complex score fall from 99 % at run 10 to 97 % at run 25?" The recommendation is the tested run with the highest predicted profit; as the correction learns the inhibition the ranking shifts by 1–2 points, within the 1 % GC noise.
- "What happens when the catalyst ages?" The protocol repeats a check run every 5th run; the model ladder claims a decay rate only when a repeated set point shows it, and the advisor reports it. In the benchmark every method ends well below the fresh-catalyst best because the catalyst really has lost ~40 % of its activity by run 25; v5 earns more than v4 but ageing is handled, not solved.
- "How long does a suggestion take?" 5–20 s on a laptop (a few thousand reactor simulations); irrelevant next to a day-long run.
- "What is placeholder?" Kinetics of both networks, prices, GC noise, the inhibition, the step limits and the flow minimum.
